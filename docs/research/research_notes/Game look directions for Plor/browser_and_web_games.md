# Visual craft of standout browser games and playful websites (for Plor)

Researched 2026-11-16. Reading note: a finding marked **(fetched)** comes from a page I opened and read; **(search summary)** means I only saw a search engine's summary of that page, so treat its details as less certain. Anything I know only from memory and could not source is listed under Gaps, marked *unverified*.

## 1. Small-team web hits: what makes each look distinctive, how it was made, and the lesson for Plor

### Takeaway
Almost every lasting browser hit was made by one to three people, and its look is one strong idea held with discipline (text only, one pixel-art mascot, one colour rule, one texture trick) rather than a rich art style. The recurring pattern is "plain page, one memorable thing that moves", which fits Plor better than the full-scene directions (pixel-art themes, 3D village) that already went too far.

### Cited Findings

**neal.fun (Neal Agarwal)**
- Agarwal is the only full-time person on neal.fun and brings in freelancers and friends per project (search summary of a paywalled interview; the full text was not readable) — [Working Theorys, "In the age of slop, craft is rebellion"](https://www.workingtheorys.com/p/the-craft-neal-agarwal)
- He makes a full-time living from ad revenue on the site, and briefly worked at MSCHF before going independent (fetched) — [Wikipedia: Neal Agarwal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neal_Agarwal)
- His stated inspiration is the "Weird Web 1.0", and he wants neal.fun to be a possible "Weird Web 2.0"; many of the games parody internet conventions or teach something (fetched) — [Wikipedia: Neal Agarwal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neal_Agarwal)
- Output is steady and small: roughly four to seven releases a year, including The Password Game (27 Jun 2023), Infinite Craft (31 Jan 2024), Stimulation Clicker (6 Jan 2025), Internet Roadtrip (6 May 2025), I'm Not a Robot (16 Sep 2025), Size of Life (10 Dec 2025), and in 2026 Sandboxels, Constellation Draw, Cursor Camp, Wiki Spy, Unusual Suspects, Spend Elon's Money and Battle Draw (9 Nov 2026) (fetched; the article's table was partly garbled, so individual dates may be off) — [Wikipedia: Neal Agarwal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neal_Agarwal)
- When a project needs art, he commissions a human specialist: palaeoartist Julius Csotonyi painted over 60 illustrations for Size of Life over five months, and Agarwal said no AI was used (fetched) — [Wikipedia: Neal Agarwal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neal_Agarwal)
- Infinite Craft's elements are generated by an LLM (reported as LLaMA 2 hosted by Together AI); the game was reported at over 300 million recipes a day, which strained the servers (search summary) — [Wikipedia: Infinite Craft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_Craft); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/this-browser-based-endless-crafting-game-starts-you-off-with-fire-and-water-but-it-quickly-escalates-to-god-the-big-bang-and-yin-yoda)

**Wordle (Josh Wardle)**
- Made by one person for his partner, with no promotion, no monetisation and no endless play; sold to the New York Times for a price "in the low seven figures" (fetched) — [Game Developer, Wardle's GDC 2022 talk](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/josh-wardle-reflects-on-the-the-unconventional-road-to-wordle-s-success)
- Wardle valued a simple, clean grid and wanted the game to take a few minutes a day; the once-a-day limit was borrowed from NYT word games and created a shared daily rhythm (fetched) — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/josh-wardle-reflects-on-the-the-unconventional-road-to-wordle-s-success)
- His wife sorted about 13,000 five-letter words by familiarity so answers would feel like known words (fetched) — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/josh-wardle-reflects-on-the-the-unconventional-road-to-wordle-s-success)

**Monkeytype (Miodec)**
- A one-person project (Jack, "Miodec", UK) worked on as his main job, with open-source contributors; two friends moderate the Discord (fetched) — [KBD.news interview, 3 years of Monkeytype](https://kbd.news/Interview-3-years-of-Monkeytype-2019.html)
- The first prototype was built on CodePen and the site launched in May 2020; the UI is text only, with no images and only a few colours, inspired by the minimalist typings.gg (fetched) — [KBD.news](https://kbd.news/Interview-3-years-of-Monkeytype-2019.html)
- As of May 2023: about 2 million registered users, about 120,000 daily users, 819 million tests started (fetched) — [KBD.news](https://kbd.news/Interview-3-years-of-Monkeytype-2019.html)
- The creator's own guess at why it took off: the pandemic, the clean minimalist design, deep customisation, being open source, constant updates and an early boost from a streamer. This is his opinion, not measured (fetched) — [KBD.news](https://kbd.news/Interview-3-years-of-Monkeytype-2019.html)
- The repo has 21.1k stars, is GPL-3.0, and its listed stack includes TypeScript, Solid, Vite, Tailwind, Sass, AnimeJS, Chart.js, Firebase, MongoDB and Redis; listed features include themes, a smooth caret and user-configurable sounds (fetched) — [GitHub: monkeytypegame/monkeytype](https://github.com/monkeytypegame/monkeytype)

**Chrome Dino**
- Built in 2014 by three people on the Chrome UX team: Sebastien Gabriel and Alan Bettes (design) and Edward Jung (engineering), who had never written a game (fetched) — [Google blog: "As the Chrome dino runs"](https://blog.google/products/chrome/chrome-dino/)
- The pixel look was not a new style: it continued Chrome's existing pixel-art error illustrations, and the cacti and desert came from the old offline page; the joke is the "prehistoric age" of no Wi-Fi (fetched) — [Google blog](https://blog.google/products/chrome/chrome-dino/)
- The team deliberately limited itself to rigid, vintage-feeling motion and cut ideas like a kick and a roar, keeping only run, duck and jump (fetched) — [Google blog](https://blog.google/products/chrome/chrome-dino/)
- The first version played badly on older Android phones and was rewritten from scratch; it reached all platforms about three months after submission (fetched) — [Google blog](https://blog.google/products/chrome/chrome-dino/)
- About 270 million plays a month, mostly from markets with unreliable or expensive mobile data (India, Brazil, Mexico, Indonesia) (fetched) — [Google blog](https://blog.google/products/chrome/chrome-dino/)

**Sandspiel (Max Bittker)**
- A solo project built in late 2018: simulation in Rust compiled to WebAssembly, rendering in WebGL, UI in React (fetched) — [Max Bittker, "Making Sandspiel"](https://maxbittker.com/making-sandspiel)
- Its distinctive look is a cheap trick: each cell stores a spare value that sets how light the pixel is, giving flat colours a subtle grain (fetched) — [Making Sandspiel](https://maxbittker.com/making-sandspiel)
- The interface was designed for touch first, avoiding multi-tap menus and two-stage selection; sharing needs no account or login (fetched) — [Making Sandspiel](https://maxbittker.com/making-sandspiel)
- Reported over 70,000 uploads and roughly a couple of thousand daily players at the time of writing (2019) (fetched) — [Making Sandspiel](https://maxbittker.com/making-sandspiel)

**A Dark Room and Universal Paperclips**
- A Dark Room began as an open-source web app; its minimal text makes the player's imagination do the visual work, with the story arriving in small increments (search summary) — [InformIT, "Interface Lessons from A Dark Room"](https://informit.com/articles/article.aspx?p=2228802)
- Universal Paperclips starts as nothing but text and numbers, with shapes added later; Frank Lantz meant it to stay small and minimal, closer to a thought experiment than a spectacle (search summary) — [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/this-clicker-game-lets-you-take-over-the-world-with-paper-clips/); [Y Combinator interview with Frank Lantz](https://blog.ycombinator.com/frank-lantz-director-of-nyus-game-center-and-creator-of-universal-paperclips)

**Cookie Clicker**

**.io games (Agar.io, Slither.io)**

**Google Doodle: Champion Island Games**

**JKLM.fun (BombParty)**

- A single creator describes having made "a couple popular multiplayer Web games at JKLM.FUN", with other work under Sparklin Labs on itch.io (search summary; indirect) — [frontendfoc.us link](https://frontendfoc.us/link/16685/web)

**Gartic Phone**
- Made by the Brazilian studio Onrizon (founded 2012, Belo Horizonte) and released in late 2020; it combines Pictionary and Telephone, and streamers drove its spread (search summary; sources conflict on the release month, October versus December 2020, and on the founder's name) — [Know Your Meme](https://knowyourmeme.com/editorials/guides/what-is-gartic-phone-and-why-do-streamers-love-it-the-multiplayer-drawing-game-explained); [Wikipedia: Broken Picture Telephone](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Broken_Picture_Telephone)

### Inferences
- **The look that lasts is a rule, not a scene.** Monkeytype (text and a few colours), Wordle (a grid and three states), A Dark Room (text), Chrome Dino (one sprite, rigid motion), Sandspiel (flat colour plus grain). None of them is "illustrated". For Plor this argues for one house rule applied everywhere (a typeface, a small palette, one motion signature) rather than themed artwork.
- **Borrow from what you already have.** The Dino's style came from Chrome's existing error art, not a new direction. Plor's equivalent raw material is letters: the host taking letters, the Fragments chunk, the clock. A look built from type and letter motion is the cheapest one to make feel native.
- **One mascot or motif is affordable; a world is not.** Dino and Lucky the cat are single characters. Champion Island needed an animation studio; the Dino needed two designers and three months. A single host character or mark, with a handful of states, is the scale a one-person team has shipped.
- **Cut the motion list.** The Dino team dropped the kick and the roar. A short, fixed set of motions (letter taken, answer locked, time running out, round won) repeated consistently reads as style; many one-off animations read as noise.
- **Commission, don't generate, if art is wanted.** Agarwal's art-heavy pieces use a named human illustrator and say so. Given Plor's AI pixel-art attempt "went too far", the neal.fun pattern is: plain page by default, one specialist commission when a piece truly needs art.
- **Touch first.** Sandspiel's regret-free decisions were about touch (no multi-tap menus). Plor's timed typing on phones makes the on-screen keyboard area the real canvas; decoration that competes with it will hurt.

### Gaps
- I could not read Neal Agarwal's process interview (paywalled) and found no reliable source for neal.fun's tech stack or traffic. *Unverified, from memory:* the site is commonly said to be built with Vue/Nuxt, and each game has its own bespoke art direction on a shared plain white index of illustrated cards.
- No sourced detail found on the look or making of: Little Alchemy, Orb.farm, Bongo Cat, Pointer Pointer, Chrome Music Lab, Paper.io, Krunker, GeoGuessr, TypeRacer, Nitro Type, Chess.com, Poki's top games, the Pac-Man doodle, or Coding for Carrots. They were named in the brief but not reached within the search budget.
- JKLM.fun publishes nothing about its design or mobile support. *Unverified, from memory:* BombParty's look is a dark page with players arranged in a circle around a bomb and an arrow, each player's typed word shown live under their avatar; the circle of live typing is the memorable image.
- Cookie Clicker's art style and its seasonal re-skins were not covered by any reliable source I found.
- Team size for Onrizon (Gartic Phone) is not published in any reliable source found.

## 2. Awwwards / FWA-style playful sites: which techniques are affordable and which are not

### Takeaway
The award-winning 3D sites are year-long efforts by specialists and their own authors name performance as the main limit; they also tend to skip ordinary HTML UI, which costs accessibility. What transfers cheaply is their sound design and small UI motion details, not the 3D.

### Cited Findings
- Bruno Simon's 2025/2026 portfolio rebuild was a solo project (plus a commissioned composer) that took just over a year; he set an end-of-2025 deadline to stop it being open-ended (fetched) — [Awwwards: Bruno's Portfolio case study](https://www.awwwards.com/brunos-portfolio-case-study.html)
- Its stack is Three.js with TSL shaders, WebGPU where supported with a fallback, and the whole world authored in Blender with Python scripting; the front-end code is MIT-licensed on GitHub (fetched) — [Awwwards case study](https://www.awwwards.com/brunos-portfolio-case-study.html)
- He says performance was the main limit on what could be built. Techniques: instancing, looping tiles for grass and water, frustum culling, merged geometry, a shared colour palette texture, compressed textures (ETC1S/UASTC) and DRACO-compressed models (fetched) — [Awwwards case study](https://www.awwwards.com/brunos-portfolio-case-study.html)
- On phones it drops effects: water blur and depth of field are turned off and shadow resolution is reduced (fetched) — [Awwwards case study](https://www.awwwards.com/brunos-portfolio-case-study.html)
- Accessibility is not discussed; he avoided traditional HTML UI in favour of 3D interface elements (fetched) — [Awwwards case study](https://www.awwwards.com/brunos-portfolio-case-study.html)
- Its UI sound is minimal: one click sound whose playback rate varies, alongside ambient and spatial audio (fetched) — [Awwwards case study](https://www.awwwards.com/brunos-portfolio-case-study.html)
- Igloo Inc won Awwwards Site of the Year and Developer Site of the Year for 2024; it was built by the studio abeto with the agency Bureaux, using Three.js, three-mesh-bvh, Svelte, GSAP and Vite, with custom tools such as a volume-data exporter and a fluid simulation (search summary) — [Awwwards: Igloo Inc case study](https://awwwards.com/igloo-inc-case-study.html); [Awwwards annual awards 2024](https://www.awwwards.com/annual-awards-2024/site-of-the-year)
- A commonly repeated mobile rule for Three.js sites is to cap the device pixel ratio (for example at 1.5) and use simpler geometry on phones (search summary of a generic guide, not a studio source) — [three.js forum thread on mobile performance](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/problems-with-performance-on-mobile-devices/23734)

### Inferences
- **Not affordable for Plor:** a WebGL/WebGPU scene as the interface. The best solo practitioner in the field needed a year, a 3D authoring pipeline and a degraded phone mode. This matches the owner's own finding that the low-poly village went too far.
- **Affordable:** (a) a single UI sound with varied pitch, as in Simon's site; (b) GSAP-style or CSS-only micro-motion on ordinary HTML elements; (c) a strict shared palette; (d) a self-imposed ship date for any look work.
- **A warning about the genre:** these sites trade away HTML UI and say nothing about accessibility. A typing game needs real inputs, focus handling and screen-reader text, so the playful layer should sit on top of normal HTML, not replace it.

### Gaps
- Active Theory, Lusion and Studio Lumio were named in the brief but not researched; no findings.
- I did not open the full Igloo Inc case study, so team size, timeline and phone behaviour are unknown.
- No primary source found for load size of any of these sites; blog claims about Bruno Simon's bundle size and frame rate were unsourced and are not reported here.

## 3. Themes and personalisation systems players love

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced model is Monkeytype's: a text-only interface where a "theme" is just a handful of colours, which makes player-selectable (and player-made) themes nearly free to build and keeps every theme legible. Lichess shows the same idea for boards and pieces, with licensing as the real constraint on adding art.

### Cited Findings
- Monkeytype's creator names extensive customisation as one of the reasons he believes the site became popular (fetched; opinion) — [KBD.news](https://kbd.news/Interview-3-years-of-Monkeytype-2019.html)
- Monkeytype themes are shared as small colour configurations and contributed through GitHub; sources disagree on the count (one docs site says 56 built-in themes, another says 400+, another "hundreds" of community themes) (search summary of third-party docs, not the official repo) — [Mintlify: Monkeytype themes guide](https://www.mintlify.com/monkeytypegame/monkeytype/guides/themes); [Mintlify: Monkeytype overview](https://mintlify.com/explore/monkeytypegame/monkeytype)
- The official repo lists "themes", a smooth caret and user-configurable sounds as features, and "add themes" as a contributor activity (fetched) — [GitHub: monkeytype](https://github.com/monkeytypegame/monkeytype)
- Lichess lets players change board theme and piece set from account settings; staff have said piece sets are added natively when the images are open source or otherwise free to use, so licensing gates what can be offered (search summary) — [Lichess forum: custom board and pieces](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/how-to-do-your-own-custom-board-pieces); [Lichess forum: how do I make my own piece set](https://lichess.org/forum/general-chess-discussion/how-do-i-make-my-own-piece-set)
- Lichess's board component (Chessground) renders pieces as SVG (search summary of a third-party docs page) — [Mintlify: Lichess frontend architecture](https://www.mintlify.com/lichess-org/lila/architecture/frontend)
- Wordle's personalisation is two switches in settings: a dark theme and a high-contrast mode that replaces green and yellow with orange and blue (search summary) — [Cult of Mac: Wordle tips](https://www.cultofmac.com/how-to/useful-wordle-hacks); [Bill Fischer, "These 2 Wordle colors undermine color…"](https://billfischer.substack.com/p/these-2-wordle-colors-undermine-color)
- Townscaper's only customisation is choosing a block colour from a small fixed palette (search summary) — [Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20211202-townscaper-demo-browser)
- Duolingo keeps its animated characters small by splitting a character into modular pieces driven by a state machine rather than hand-animating every reaction; the stated constraint was a file small enough for Android, iOS and the web (search summary) — [Duolingo blog: world character visemes](https://blog.duolingo.com/world-character-visemes); [Rive blog: Lily video call](https://rive.app/blog/duolingo-s-ai-powered-video-call-brings-lily-to-life)
- Rive's own marketing claims Duolingo cut animation file size 15x by adopting Rive, with no baseline given (search summary; vendor claim) — [Rive: Rive as a Lottie alternative](https://www.rive.app/blog/rive-as-a-lottie-alternative)

### Inferences
- **A colour-token theme system is the cheapest route to "personal".** If Plor's look is built from type, a few colours and motion, then a theme is a handful of named colours, the same shape Monkeytype uses. That also gives dark mode and a high-contrast mode as two more entries in the same list rather than separate features.
- **Themes work when the base is plain.** Monkeytype and Lichess can offer many themes because the interface carries no illustration to redraw. The earlier pixel-art themes failed in the opposite direction: each theme needed new art.
- **Personalise the player's own marks first.** In these products the customised thing is what the player touches all the time (caret, board, pieces, block colour). For Plor that is the typed text, the caret, the answer chip and the result card, more than the page background.
- **Curate before opening up.** Lichess's licensing gate and Wordle's two switches suggest starting with a small designed set; community themes are a later step that needs moderation.
- **If a character is wanted, make it a state machine.** Duolingo's approach (few parts, a handful of states) is what keeps a mascot cheap; it maps to the small fixed motion list in section 1.

### Gaps
- No reliable current count of Monkeytype themes, and no official description of its theme format, was confirmed. *Unverified, from memory:* a Monkeytype theme is about ten colour values (background, main, caret, sub, text, error and so on) and the site lets users build and share a custom theme by link.
- Chess.com piece sets, Discord themes, Duolingo avatars and Tetris skins were named in the brief but not researched; no findings.
- I found no data (surveys, retention figures) showing that themes increase retention anywhere; the evidence is creators' opinions and the features' popularity.

## 4. Which of these get shared because of their look, and what triggers the sharing

### Takeaway
The clearest evidence is Wordle: the shared object was a tiny abstract picture of the player's own result that spoiled nothing and puzzled outsiders, and it was invented by a player, not designed. What gets shared is the player's story in the game's visual language, not the game's artwork.

### Cited Findings
- Wardle did not invent the emoji grid: a player in New Zealand typed her result out with the emoji keyboard, others copied her, and he then built it in (fetched) — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/josh-wardle-reflects-on-the-the-unconventional-road-to-wordle-s-success); [The Spinoff](https://thespinoff.co.nz/media/13-01-2022/those-coloured-boxes-on-twitter-are-new-zealands-fault)
- He says the grid tells the story of a solve, and its mysterious look worked as an in-group signal that made others ask what it was (fetched) — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/josh-wardle-reflects-on-the-the-unconventional-road-to-wordle-s-success)
- He left the link and game name out of the share on purpose, feeling a link would clash with the clean look and uneasy about pushing players to recruit (fetched) — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/josh-wardle-reflects-on-the-the-unconventional-road-to-wordle-s-success)
- The grid lets a player show how they did without ruining the puzzle for others (search summary) — [RNZ interview with Josh Wardle](https://www.rnz.co.nz/national/programmes/the-weekend/audio/2018826546/josh-wardle-the-power-of-wordle)
- Wordle's popularity rose in December 2021 after the copy-results-as-emoji feature was added (search summary) — [Wikipedia: Wordle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle)
- Sandspiel's sharing is the player's own creation posted to a public board with no account, which gave every upload an immediate audience; the community produced remixes, puzzles and paintings (fetched) — [Making Sandspiel](https://maxbittker.com/making-sandspiel)
- Agar.io and Gartic Phone spread through video creators and streamers rather than screenshots (search summary) — [Wikipedia: .io games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/.io_games); [Know Your Meme: Gartic Phone](https://knowyourmeme.com/editorials/guides/what-is-gartic-phone-and-why-do-streamers-love-it-the-multiplayer-drawing-game-explained)
- Infinite Craft's shareable moment is a personal discovery (the game's "first discovery" of a new element), produced by text, not art (search summary) — [Wikipedia: Infinite Craft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_Craft)

### Inferences
- **Design the result, not the wallpaper.** For Plor, the shareable object is a compact, spoiler-free picture of a round: for Offbeat, seven marks showing which answers dodged the host and which letters were taken; for Fragments, seven marks of survived or failed chunks. It should be recognisable at thumbnail size and say nothing of the day's answers.
- **Abstract beats branded.** Wordle's grid carried no logo or link, and its oddness did the marketing. A Plor share that is a distinctive shape or glyph row in the house palette would follow that pattern; a branded card with a URL is the conventional alternative Wardle rejected.
- **Watch what beta players do by hand.** The grid came from a player. During the closed beta, how testers describe or screenshot their results is the best brief for the share format.
- **Two kinds of sharing need different looks.** Text or emoji results spread in chats; clips spread through streamers when the screen is readable by a spectator (Gartic Phone, Agar.io, BombParty's circle). If Plor ever has live rooms, the "whole game legible in one glance" property matters more than polish.
- **Player-made things get shared.** Sandspiel uploads and Infinite Craft discoveries are both "look what I made or found". A chosen theme on the result card would put a small piece of the player into every share.

### Gaps
- No reliable source found for how Connections, Framed, Heardle, Worldle or Contexto designed their share formats.
- No measured data found on share rates by format (emoji text versus image card) for any game.
- Nothing sourced on whether neal.fun games are shared for their look versus their premise. *Unverified, from memory:* Password Game and Stimulation Clicker spread mainly as screen recordings of escalating chaos, which is motion and premise rather than art style.

## 5. Load time, phone performance and accessibility lessons

### Takeaway
The hits that travel furthest are tiny and run on bad phones; their makers' recurring regret is spending performance headroom on looks. Accessibility in this field is mostly handled by a plain text-and-colour base plus explicit modes (high contrast, screen-reader layout), and colour-only signalling is the common failure.

### Cited Findings
- Chrome Dino's first version ran poorly on older Android devices and had to be rewritten; most of its 270 million monthly plays come from markets with unreliable or costly mobile data (fetched) — [Google blog](https://blog.google/products/chrome/chrome-dino/)
- Sandspiel's author regrets raising the canvas from 250×250 to 300×300 because many devices then could not hold 60 FPS, and advises keeping headroom for weak devices (fetched) — [Making Sandspiel](https://maxbittker.com/making-sandspiel)
- Sandspiel's compiled simulation is small (the WebAssembly grew from 66 KB to 84 KB when optimised for speed), and the author could not debug iPhone-specific graphics problems without a Mac (fetched) — [Making Sandspiel](https://maxbittker.com/making-sandspiel)
- Bruno Simon's site disables effects and lowers shadow quality on mobile and names performance as the main constraint (fetched) — [Awwwards case study](https://www.awwwards.com/brunos-portfolio-case-study.html)
- JKLM.fun's FAQ notes some players get disconnected because of how their ISP handles websockets (fetched) — [JKLM.fun FAQ](https://jklm.fun/faq)
- Monkeytype's first attempt at a typing site failed partly because of poor accessibility, by its creator's account (fetched) — [KBD.news](https://kbd.news/Interview-3-years-of-Monkeytype-2019.html)

### Inferences
- **Motion on a timed typing game must never cost input latency.** The makers' regrets are all "we spent headroom on looks". For Plor, any look work should be CSS transforms and opacity on a few elements, tested on an old Android phone with the keyboard open, before it is judged on a desktop.
- **Test on the weakest phone first, and on a real iPhone.** Both the Dino rewrite and Sandspiel's iPhone blind spot came from testing late on the hard devices.
- **State needs a second signal besides colour.** "Letter taken", "matched the host", "valid word" should each carry a shape, icon, position or motion as well as a colour, so high-contrast and colour-blind play is the default rather than a hidden setting.
- **Put accessibility switches where they are found.** Lichess puts its screen-reader mode first on the page; Wordle's buried toggle draws criticism. Plor's existing Reduce motion setting (already planned before the polish freeze) fits the same pattern and matters more once motion is added.
- **A plain HTML base is the accessibility strategy.** The accessible products here (Monkeytype's text UI, Lichess's simplified layout) keep real text and controls; the 3D sites do not address it at all.

### Gaps
- No published page-weight or load-time figures found for Lichess, Monkeytype, Wordle, neal.fun or JKLM.fun; measuring them directly with browser dev tools would be needed.
- No source found on how any of these handle the phone on-screen keyboard covering the play area, which is Plor's specific risk for timed typing.
- No source found on reduced-motion handling in any of the surveyed games.
